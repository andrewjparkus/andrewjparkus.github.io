The model WNBA six-factor RAPM, re-solved every game-day

Every WNBA possession from 2022 through 2026 goes into one regularized plus-minus solve, re-run as of each game-day. The output is six factors that add up to a single net rating, in points per 100 possessions. This page says what that number is, how it is fit, and — at some length — what it cannot tell you.

This board is not on the NBA scale

The men's version of this site puts NCAA, G-League and NBA players on one axis. It can do that because thousands of players move between those leagues, and a player who appears in two of them at known ability is what lets you fit the mapping between their scales. The men's network is fit on 826 college→NBA movers and 795 G-League→NBA movers.

No player moves between the WNBA and the NBA. There is no mover population, so there is nothing to fit a mapping from, and there never will be. That is a structural fact about the two leagues, not a limitation of the data we happen to have.

So a WNBA +5.0 on this site means “five points per 100 possessions better than an average WNBA opponent”. It is not comparable to a +5.0 on the men's board, and the two are deliberately kept in separate files so that no leaderboard can accidentally mix them.

The six factors

Net rating is the sum of six pieces — three offensive, three defensive. They are solved separately and add up exactly, with no residual term:

  • oTS / dTS — shooting. Built from six underlying atoms: rim, mid-range and three-point conversion, rim and three-point shot selection, and free throws.
  • oTOV / dTOV — turnovers, valued by what the lost possession was worth.
  • oSC / dSC — second chances. This is second-chance value, not a rebounding count.

Defence is sign-flipped everywhere on this site, so positive always means good.

How hard each factor is shrunk

Plus-minus needs regularization or it hands a bench player a wild rating off fifty possessions. Each factor carries its own penalty, and the relative strengths are inherited from the men's model, where they were tuned against out-of-time scoring margin.

The open question was whether the WNBA wants those penalties scaled up or down as a group. Smaller league, shorter seasons, fewer possessions per player — the intuition says shrink harder. We measured it instead: seven forward-time cuts, 1,063 held-out games, multipliers from 0.125× to 16×. The curve has a clean interior minimum at 1.0× — the same penalties the men's model uses — with game-margin RMSE of 12.61 there against 12.64 at 0.75× and 12.62 at 1.5×. Possession-level error agrees.

Honest reading: the curve is flat near its minimum. A paired bootstrap of 1.0× against either neighbour crosses zero (4/7 and 3/7 folds). So the right statement is “no evidence the WNBA wants a different shrinkage”, not “the WNBA is proven to want exactly this”. It is measured, not assumed, and it is not resolved.

Three modes, and the one that is missing

Pure RAPM is the default: the lineup data with no prior at all. Box prior is a separate box-score model of the same six factors, fit only on WNBA box stats, shown at its own scale. Prior-informed is the solve run with that box model as its shrinkage target instead of zero.

What Prior-informed actually is

Regularized plus-minus works by pulling every player toward a default. Ordinarily that default is zero — “assume average until the possessions say otherwise”. Prior-informed replaces zero with what this player's box score predicts, per factor, per game-day. The prior goes inside the solve, not on top of it, so it is not a blend: a player with a lot of on-court evidence has a likelihood strong enough to overrule their prior, and a twenty-minute rookie does not.

That gradient is measurable, and it matters, because “this board is mostly its prior” is a fair criticism of some public metrics. On the last game-day, sorting the 300 rated players into five equal groups by possession exposure, the share of each player's rating that comes from the prior runs from 0.66 for the least-exposed fifth down to 0.20 for the most-exposed — 0.285 across the league weighted by exposure. Worked ends: Alyssa Thomas, with 216 games of box score behind her, has a prior of +2.01 and the possessions shrink away half of it. A player with a hundred possessions keeps almost all of hers.

It predicts real games better. Same 42 walk-forward two-week blocks, same 1,026 held-out games, no leakage: out-of-time game-margin RMSE falls from 12.551 to 12.483, with a game-clustered paired bootstrap of +0.068, 95% CI [0.019, 0.118]. That excludes zero, so the improvement is real — and it is also small — the interval runs from about a fiftieth of a point of margin to about an eighth — and it is one league, one corpus, one walk-forward. Accuracy on the winner goes 68.6% → 69.4%.

Two things about this board that look like bugs and are not.
(1) It is not centred on zero. The box maps are fit weighted by possessions, so their prediction for a rarely-used player sits below the possession-weighted average. The league mean net moves from −0.04 on Pure to −0.18 here. Read the ordering, not the level, and do not compute a replacement-level line off it.
(2) The biggest movers are thin-exposure players moving DOWN, not stars moving up. Cassandre Prosper goes from +0.05 to −2.54 on 22 box games, because her box score is the worst in the corpus and there are not enough possessions to argue. If a rookie looks harshly rated here, that is the prior, by construction. Pure is one click away and has no opinion about her box score.

Two honest limits. It is box-only — the men's version of this mode also folds in a commercial plus-minus metric, and no WNBA equivalent exists to fold in, so this is a strictly thinner prior wearing the same name. And the defensive half barely moves: of the eight underlying quantities the solve estimates, only two carry any out-of-sample defensive signal in a box score (turnovers and free throws), so only those two are wired up. Second-chance defence has a prior of nothing and is left shrinking toward zero, which is why dSC is almost identical between Pure and Prior-informed. Nearly all of the margin improvement above is offensive.

The mode that is still missing

The men's site has a fourth mode, Latent, which fuses a player's separate league stints into one continuous curve. With a single league there is nothing to fuse; it would be an identity function wearing a meaningful name. It is absent rather than aliased.

How accurate is it?

The honest answer is that we do not know as well as we know it for the men's board, and the gap is not close.

The men's model is pulled on from four independent directions: a commercial plus-minus metric, a second public one, Vegas closing lines, and twenty-five seasons of realized game margins. When it drifts, something external disagrees and we find out.

This board has none of that. There is no public WNBA plus-minus metric we are licensed to publish against, no odds ledger, no translation anchor, and five seasons of history. The only accuracy statement that is fully ours to make is the one above: out-of-time game-margin RMSE of 12.61 across seven forward cuts and 1,063 held-out games, against 14.14 for predicting nothing. That is a real edge and it is also the entire external check that exists. (A second, finer-grained harness — 42 two-week walk-forward blocks over 1,026 games — is what the Prior-informed comparison above is measured on: 12.551 for Pure against 12.483 for Prior-informed. Two harnesses, two slightly different numbers for the same board; neither is a third opinion about it.)

What this means for a reader: treat the ordering near the top of the board as informative and the third decimal as noise. A wrong choice somewhere in this pipeline would produce a board that still looked entirely plausible, because nothing outside it is currently in a position to object.

Projecting the rest of 2026

The Rest of 2026 page simulates the 128 regular-season games remaining after the board's last day (28 July – 24 September) plus the playoffs, 10,000 times. The chain is short and every link is measured on WNBA data only:

  • Team strength is the minute-weighted sum of the five on-floor board ratings, with minutes taken from each team's last 10 games and normalized to 200 player-minutes per team per game — a WNBA game is 40 minutes with five on the floor, not the NBA's 240. The published column is centred on the fifteen-team mean, so 0.0 is an average 2026 team; the board's own level (+4.91) is subtracted. Only differences reach the game model, so that shift changes nothing — rerun at the same seed, every projected win and every title chance comes back bit-identical.
  • The game model is fit on this board and borrows nothing from the men's: home advantage +1.83 points, a strength-to-margin slope of 1.071, and a residual sd of 12.52. The slope above 1 is expected — a shrunk board under-disperses against realised margins.
  • The variance is split, not stacked. That 12.52 already contains the board's own error about each team, so simulating a season with all of it as per-game noise would make win totals too narrow. The persistent per-team piece is measured off weekly forward cuts (52 team-seasons) at 1.40 points and taken out, leaving 12.37 per game. Single-game spread is preserved; season spread is not understated.
  • The bracket is assumed: an eight-team field out of fifteen, seeded league-wide, best-of-3 / 5 / 7. The format is verified — of all 40,320 possible seedings of the 2025 field, three reproduce the 2025 playoffs game for game and all three imply the correct champion — but that 2026 keeps an eight-team field in an expansion year is an assumption, and the page says so above the table.

As of 22 July: Minnesota 33.1 projected wins and a 37.6% title chance, Las Vegas 30.6 and 25.2%, Dallas 29.5 and 13.3%.

Three caveats, in order of how much they matter.
(1) There is no injury or availability feed anywhere in this project. A player who has missed their team's last ten games enters at nearly zero expected minutes even if they are about to return. The loudest live case is Minnesota's Napheesa Collier, who has played exactly one 2026 game. Rather than guess, the page publishes the exact derivative — what ten more minutes per game is worth in wins — for every player the window is under-crediting. For Collier that derivative is 0.32 wins per ten minutes, so restoring her whole 20-minute gap is about 0.64 wins.
(2) Team strength is frozen at 22 July and does not drift, age or respond to a trade over the remaining eight weeks.
(3) The projection runs on the Prior-informed board, with a game model re-fit on that same board — a less-shrunk board wants a smaller slope, and it got one (1.144 → 1.071). Reusing the Pure slope instead would have widened every band by about 6%, so the two are now chosen from one key and the builder refuses to run a mismatched pair. The gain is real but small, and it is not consistent: the Prior-informed board wins only 24 of the 42 walk-forward blocks. Running the whole projection on Pure instead moves no team's wins by more than 0.4 and changes neither the order nor the favourite.

The data

2022–2026 regular season plus 2022–2025 playoffs: 1,263 games, 208,082 possessions, 357 players, with lineups reconstructed from event-level play-by-play. There is no 2026 postseason yet.

Earlier seasons are absent for a specific reason: WNBA play-by-play before 2022 has not had its on-floor lineups reconstructed, and plus-minus without lineups is not possible. That is a data-engineering project, not a modelling choice.

One consequence worth naming: the solve uses a five-year trailing window, and the whole corpus is 1,538 days. So the window is currently inert — every day's solve sees all prior history. It will start to bite once there is more than five years of it.

Possession counts run about 3% above the public reference. It is stable across all five seasons, which is the signature of uniform dilution rather than a concentrated defect, and it is absorbed by the WNBA-internal scale — but it does mean raw per-100 rates from this board should not be quoted against another source's.

Limits

  • No cross-league comparison, ever. See the first section.
  • No minutes forecast. The projection uses a ten-game recency window in place of one. Widening or narrowing that window is a measured dead end — every alternative from three games to season-long lands inside the noise — so the missing piece is an availability model, not a bigger window.
  • No individual player projection. The men's site projects five years of each player's trajectory. That needs an aging curve fit on far more league-seasons than five, so there is no player forecast here, only a team one.
  • The bands on the Explorer describe what already happened, not what will. They are wide, and reading them as a forecast interval overstates them substantially. (The win-total bands on the Rest of 2026 page are a different object — those are forecast intervals, from the simulation.)
  • Prior-informed carries the same error bars as Pure, to within 0.006 across the whole board. That is not a copy-paste bug: a ridge's posterior standard error depends on how much evidence a player has, not on what the estimate was shrunk toward. The prior moves the number, not the uncertainty about it.
  • Defence is weakly identified, as it is in every plus-minus model. Second-chance defence in particular carries close to no independent box signal.
  • The board is refreshed in batches, not nightly. The current corpus ends 22 July 2026.
  • One factor is quarantined. The three-point shot-selection atom sits on a different scale here than its men's counterpart, because the underlying feed bakes a per-season constant into it. It cancels between offence and defence, so net ratings are unaffected — but the oTS/dTS split can shift by up to about 0.13 for a given player, and that atom should not be read as the same quantity as the men's.
