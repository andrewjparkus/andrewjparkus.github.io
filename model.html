The model six-factor RAPM, re-solved every game-day

A six-factor RAPM re-solved as of every game-day across the NBA, G-League and NCAA on one NBA-equivalent scale, every value with a confidence interval. Ratings are descriptive — what a player has already done — unless a page is labelled a forward projection.

The six factors

Three offensive, three defensive, each in points per 100 possessions, sign-aligned so positive is always good.

FactorWhat it measuresPositive means
oTS Scoring efficiency — true shooting, including free-throw value scores efficiently
oTOV Ball security protects the ball
oSC Second chances created — putbacks and kickouts off the offensive glass, not a rebound rate creates extra possessions
dTS Shot defense — making opponents shoot worse suppresses opponent efficiency
dTOV Forcing turnovers forces turnovers
dSC Second chances denied denies opponent extra shots

Net is exactly the sum of the six, never a separate solve. The decomposition follows Russell Thomas's additive RAPM, and the ridge solver core is his reference implementation.

How hard each factor is shrunk

A ridge solve needs a shrinkage strength, an alpha, for every factor, on offense and defense separately. Shrink too little and a player who happened to be on the floor for a hot stretch looks great. Shrink too much and everyone collapses toward average. The sixteen alphas we use were tuned by Russell Thomas against out-of-time scoring margin on 2021-26 NBA possessions.

They were fitted on NBA possessions, so carrying them into another league is an assumption rather than a neutral default. We checked it league by league, each against its own held-out games: hold out a 90-day window after a cut date, re-solve on everything before it, then score the summed factors against what actually happened. College came back wanting materially less shrinkage, and now runs its own setting.

LeagueShrinkage now usedWhy
NBA the tuned alphas, unchanged they were fitted here; a different solver, and not re-tested
G-League the tuned alphas, unchanged its own best setting is 1.25×, worth +0.006, which is inside noise
College 0.375×, about 2.7× less +0.10 points of game-margin error, on every test split

The G-League's +0.006 is a number to ignore. Its confidence interval crosses zero and it wins four of the eight test splits, which is what a coin flip looks like. College is the opposite: the interval excludes zero and it wins eight of eight. Across all seventy ways of splitting the eight test seasons in half, choosing the scale on one half beat the NBA-tuned alphas on the other half seventy times out of seventy, and the same answer comes back when the test dates move from mid-January to mid-February.

Why college is the exception. Talent spread. College runs from future lottery picks down to walk-ons, and shrinking that hard erases differences that are real: how much player spread a setting preserves and how well it predicts game margins rank in exactly the same order. The G-League inverts this. Widening its spread makes margins worse, because everyone there is a fringe-NBA player and the extra spread is measurement noise, not talent. So this is not a knob with one right value. It depends on how far apart the players in a league actually are.

One thing we deliberately did not do: let all sixteen alphas move independently per league. That buys a little more on paper, but it lands on a different answer depending on which seasons you tune with. Two equally good tuning runs disagreed by as much as 48× on individual factors while scoring the same. A single scale per league is the part that reproduces.

The NBA is the league we did not re-test: it runs a different solver, and the tuned alphas were fitted on its possessions to begin with. Every figure above is scored on a league's own held-out games, so it says the ratings fit that league better, not that they travel better across leagues.

Putting three leagues on one scale

A college or G-League player is first rated inside his own league, against the players he actually shared a floor with. That rating then has to be moved onto the NBA scale, or the three leagues could not share a chart. The move is a straight-line conversion, a slope and an offset, fitted separately for each of the six factors.

That conversion was fitted on one quantity and applied to another. The slope and offset were estimated from players' career-long averages. They were then applied to the daily rating, which looks back over a rolling five-year window and weights recent games more heavily. Those are two different numbers about the same player, so the conversion came out at the wrong level.

How wrong is measurable directly, without any modelling. Some players appear in a lower league and in the NBA in the same season: a G-League call-up, a rookie whose college season ended in March. For those players the two ratings can be put side by side. Their pre-NBA rating sat about two and a half points above what the same players produced in the NBA that same season. Development and selection cannot explain a gap inside one season; the conversion can.

LeagueSame-season gap, before Same-season gap, after
College+2.63 +0.02
G-League+2.28 +0.40

6,967 player-seasons in which a player appeared in both a lower league and the NBA. Points per 100 possessions; positive means the pre-NBA rating read too high.

The fix was to re-fit the conversion on the daily rating itself, matching each pre-NBA reading to the closest NBA evidence in time rather than to a career average. Two other things moved with it. The jump in a player's line when he crosses leagues fell from 2.08 points to 1.18, a 43% reduction, and that is before any smoothing is applied. And the conversion's own error bar shrank on every one of the six factors, so this is a better fit to the data, not the same fit slid down the page.

What this changes on the site. Every college and G-League rating is now roughly 2.5 points lower than it was before. NBA ratings are unchanged.

Limits

  • The error bars measure today, not next year. A band says how tightly the data pins down what a player has already done; it is not a forecast.
  • Game margins sit near the noise floor. Almost no modelling change moves them, and they are scored on the minutes players actually logged, so it is rating quality given usage, not a pre-game forecast.
  • Defense is the soft spot. The inputs can't see contested shots or rim protection, so the defensive factors travel worst.
  • Cross-league jumps stay uncertain. The college and G-League conversions are heavily attenuated, so draft bands are wide by construction.
  • Rookie projections are the weakest thing here. Turning a prospect's college rating into a first NBA season improves on simply predicting the average rookie by 0.022 points of error: a real edge, but a small one. Treat a rookie band as a rough range, not a call.
  • Coverage gaps. No public play-by-play before 1996-97 (NBA) or 2016-17 (G-League), and NBA feeds record no steals or blocks from 1997 to 2013.
  • College starts at 2013-14. Earlier seasons exist upstream but are not usable: the pre-2014 college feed files fouls and turnovers under one label, so every foul was counted as a possession that ended in a turnover, about 175 possessions a game against a true 135. Fixing the labelling recovers 2009-13, but 2006-08 record no turnovers at all, and no season before 2014 records which free throw in a trip a shot was, so we cut the era rather than publish numbers built on invented possessions. The earliest college days are also thin, because a five-year window has little history to draw on before about 2016.

All values in points per 100 possessions on the NBA-equivalent scale. Measured ratings carry measurement error bars; cross-league values carry an extra, non-shrinkable translation uncertainty.
