A daily, multi-league six-factor RAPM — a reader's guide

For readers who know the traditional six-factor RAPM decomposition: what's new here, how it works, and what it says — with numbers.

The one-liner

Traditional six-factor RAPM gives one number per player per season: the six factors — oTS, oTOV, oSC on offense, dTS, dTOV, dSC on defense (points per 100, summing to net). This model re-computes that decomposition as of every game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA) on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value with a confidence interval.

So instead of "Jokić was +8 in 2025-26," you get his rating as a daily curve across his career and through the playoffs, beside a college freshman's curve on the same axis — each with error bars.

The sixth factor. What older boards called "oREB/dREB" is here second-chance value (oSC/dSC): points added after the offense's own miss — putbacks, kickouts off the offensive glass, and on defense the prevention of those second chances. It is not a rebound rate (Jokić grades dSC ≈ +2.0, Wembanyama ≈ +0.9).

Four things that are different

Daily, not seasonal. Every game-day, the model re-solves the full ridge regression as of that day — a smooth curve, not a step function. The NBA series spans regular season and playoffs with no break: each day sees only games dated ≤ itself, so a star's net flows continuously from April into June.

Multi-league and self-consistent. College and G-League players are translated onto the NBA scale, so a Duke sophomore and an All-NBA center are directly comparable (more below — it's the most interesting part).

Error bars on everything. Each factor, each day, carries a confidence interval that widens with few possessions (early career, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up) and narrows as the sample grows. Cross-league numbers carry an extra, non-shrinkable uncertainty for the leap between leagues.

Smooth, no between-season jumps. Day-to-day the median change in net is about 0.02 points; across a full offseason, under 0.8 — by construction, not smoothing.

How a "daily re-solve" works

Each day's value is a full RAPM: a time-decayed ridge over every possession in the trailing ~6 years (a 2,190-day window for the NBA), recent games weighted more — a 700-day half-life, so a game's weight is 2^(−age/700). "As of day D" just means feeding it games dated ≤ D — there is no carried-over state, so every day stands alone. (Planting a future game moves the as-of estimate by exactly 0.0; the build is causally clean by construction.)

The ridge is solved in three column groups with separate penalties — offense and defense at α = 3000, plus a small α = 10 group of garbage-time "rubber-band" nuisance columns — over a decay-weighted, whitened design. It is a maximum-a-posteriori estimate under a Gaussian prior on each player's coefficient: the pure series shrinks toward zero; the prior-informed series shrinks three factors toward a box-score prior instead (next section).

That as-of-day independence is a gift: the whole thing is embarrassingly parallel, and each league's giant possession matrix is built once and re-sliced per day. The NBA design alone is ~7.1 million possession-rows × ~5,800 player-columns. The NBA has ~6,080 game-days; with the other leagues it's ~10,200 league-days × five ridge solves each — on the order of ~51,000 solves, each over a million-plus possessions.

Net is its own solve. The six factors are assembled from four ridge "leaf" metrics (oTS = ALT_EFG_VALUE + ALT_FT, oTOV/dTOV from turnover value, oSC/dSC from clean second chances). Their sum, sum6 = ORAPM + DRAPM, is shown as the player's offense/defense split, while the headline net is a separate net-RAPM solve of the same possessions; the two agree to within a few hundredths (|net − sum6| peaks at ~0.5).

Three views of the same day: pure, prior-informed, box prior

The explorer toggles each chart between three daily series. They share dates, leagues, and error bars; they differ only in what the three "shrink-toward" targets are.

Pure RAPM — the canonical, prior-free solve. Every factor shrinks toward zero, exactly as a textbook regularized RAPM does. This is the series tuned to reproduce the public DataBallR ridge (below), and it is the reference the other two are measured against.

Prior-informed (the default) — the same solve, but three of the six factors shrink toward an informative, daily, multi-league box-score prior instead of toward zero. The box prior is a per-player skill estimate built by regressing predictive box rates onto the engine's own RAPM, re-computed as of every game-day from all of a player's games across NCAA, G-League, and the NBA up to that day. The blend is exposure-weighted, (1−M)·lineup + M·box with M = α/(weff+α): with little on-court data a rating leans on the box estimate; as possessions pile up the lineup data takes over — so a rookie, a fresh call-up, or a player early in a season starts from a sensible place rather than from replacement level, and a cross-league prospect's NBA prior is informed by what he did in college or the G-League.

The prior is applied only to the three factors where the daily box carries the most signal, chosen by out-of-sample testing (does an as-of-day rating predict next season better?): second-chance offense (oSC), shot defense (dTS), and forcing turnovers (dTOV). The other three — shooting (oTS), ball-security (oTOV), and defensive rebounding (dSC) — are left at the pure solve; a daily box built from traditional box stats (no shot-tracking) simply doesn't predict those three well enough to help thin-data players. (This is the opposite split from the old constant prior, which leaned on shooting.) It is a deliberately modest adjustment: for established players it barely moves the rating, and even for thin-data players the out-of-sample edge over the pure solve is small — the prior's clearer value is being time-varying, causal, and multi-league (so a rookie's NBA prior reflects his actual college / G-League box, on every day, instead of a single career number). Prior-informed and pure are identical on oTS, oTOV, dSC and on the headline net; the toggle lets you compare the pure solve against the box-nudged one for the three factors it touches.

Box prior — the prior itself, on its own: a daily, multi-league curve with a confidence band, showing the box-score skill the model expected of a player before any lineup data, re-estimated every game-day from his box profile across all three leagues. It is no longer a flat reference — it rises and falls as a player's box game develops, runs continuously across a college→G-League→NBA career (each league translated onto the NBA-equivalent scale), and widens its band where the estimate is thin. Compared against Pure and Prior-informed it shows where a player started and how far the on-court data has moved him: for a developed star the box prior sits well below his measured lineup RAPM (a conservative anchor the on-court data amplifies); for a rookie the two start close and diverge as the lineup signal accrues.

Does it match a known reference?

The pure NBA scale is tuned to reproduce DataBallR's six-factor RAPM (the public 6-year, time-decay, pure-lineup ridge), anchored at the regular-season end (DataBallR is regular-season only; by causality that solve is playoff-free). The interesting part: what transfers between leagues?

Here the multi-league model earns its keep. We don't assume college production means the same thing as NBA production — we measure it, using two-league players (786 who played both G-League and NBA, 1,276 who went college → NBA). For each factor we fit how lower-league value predicts NBA value: the slope is how much transfers, the correlation R how reliably.

Factor NCAA→NBA slope R G-League→NBA slope R
oTS (shooting) 0.24 0.22 0.10 0.11
oTOV (ball security) 0.08 0.12 0.38 0.38
oSC (offensive 2nd-chance) 0.15 0.15 0.41 0.34
dTS (shot defense) 0.26 0.22 0.31 0.21
dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.13 0.14 0.77 0.47
dSC (defensive 2nd-chance) 0.17 0.12 0.30 0.22

The model's whole personality is in this table:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely moves your NBA number — the competition gap is enormous. A college player who is +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Shooting transfers weakly (G-League R ≈ 0.1, college only modestly): efficiency against lower-league defenses says little about scoring on NBA ones.
  • Hustle/instinct carries over best — forcing turnovers (G-League R 0.47) and generating offensive second chances (G-League R 0.34) reflect motor more than skill-against-weaker-competition.
  • Defensive second-chance barely transfers from college (dSC R = 0.12): out-working weaker college bigs on the glass tells you little about the NBA. We let the data decide this.

The translation also carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate) per factor — the league jump is genuinely uncertain, so cross-league error bars are wide and never shrink to zero, however many college games a player has. That honesty is the point: it's why a future MVP's lone college season can sit near replacement level on the NBA axis and still be "consistent" with stardom — the band is that wide.

Confidence intervals

Every factor on every day carries a standard error, se = 100·√σ̂² / √Weff, where Weff is the decay-weighted possession exposure that day. So the band widens with few effective possessions — a rookie's first weeks, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up — and narrows as minutes accumulate. At high exposure the net band runs ±1.5 or so; on a thin sample it balloons past ±5. Translated (college/G-League) factors add the non-shrinkable Var_translate floor in quadrature, so their bands stay permanently wide — the league jump is uncertain in a way more games can't fix.

What it looks like for real players

A full career as a smooth curve. LeBron James across 23 seasons rises from a replacement-level rookie (wide CI) through a Miami-era peak, then a gentle age-41 decline — no season boundaries, no jumps, the error band tightening as his minutes pile up and widening as his role shrinks.

The cross-league reality check. Cooper Flagg, the 2025 No. 1 pick, was elite in college — yet his Duke season translates to roughly replacement level (about 0.0 NBA-equivalent, heavily discounted), and his actual NBA rookie year has hovered near −1.3. The picture tells the story a single number hides: dominant college production maps to "promising prospect," and the real NBA learning curve is steep — all on one axis, NCAA handing off to NBA. Because college now reaches back to 2005-06, pre-2021 college→NBA careers (an SGA-at-Kentucky type) appear in full.

A three-league journey. A two-way player like Jake LaRavia shows all three colors on one chart — NCAA → NBA with G-League stints interleaved — every leg on the same scale, hovering near replacement level, exactly where a fringe rotation player belongs. Flip him to Box prior and you see the same three-league curve from the box-score side: what his box stats projected him to be at each step, his college and G-League box feeding the early-NBA prior before the lineup data took over — a fitted curve with its own confidence band, not a flat line.

That's roughly 42 million player-day rows, each with six factors, a net, and confidence intervals. College seasons 2005-06 through 2012-13 have thinner play-by-play coverage (21–67% of scheduled games) and are flagged reduced_sample to be down-weighted, not trusted blindly; coverage is full (≥85%) from 2013-14 on.

What's next (lower-value): NBA pre-1996 and pre-2005 college have no public play-by-play, so the possession-level RAPM can't extend there — those eras would need box-only strength priors rather than the daily series.
