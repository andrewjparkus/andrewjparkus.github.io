The one-liner

Classic six-factor RAPM gives one number per player per season — six components (points per 100 possessions, summing to net) split across offense and defense. This model re-computes that decomposition as of every game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA) on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value with a confidence interval.

So instead of "Jokic was +8 in 2025-26," you get his rating as a daily curve across his career and through the playoffs, beside a college freshman's curve on the same axis — each with error bars. On the reference (pure) DataBallR scale, net +5 is clear All-Star level; the league centers near 0, the very best sit around +7 to +9 (one MVP-tier outlier above that), and the negative tail near −7. (The Explorer's default prior-informed series runs hotter still — see the reference match.)

The sixth factor, renamed. What older boards called "oREB/dREB" is here second-chance value (oSC/dSC): points added after the offense's own miss — putbacks and kickouts off the offensive glass, and on defense the prevention of those second chances. It is not a rebound rate (Jokic grades dSC ≈ +2.6, Wembanyama ≈ +1.1).

The six factors

Three offensive, three defensive, each in points per 100 possessions on the NBA-equivalent scale. Offense and defense are sign-aligned so that positive is always good.

The decomposition itself is not ours: it follows Russell Thomas's additive RAPM decomposition, and the ridge solver core is a vendored, byte-identical copy of his reference implementation. This project is the daily, multi-league product layer built on top of that engine.

FactorWhat it measuresPositive means
oTS Offensive true-shooting value (EFG + FT value) scores efficiently
oTOV Offensive turnover value — ball security protects the ball
oSC Offensive second-chance value (putbacks/kickouts off the o-glass) creates extra possessions
dTS Defensive true-shooting — making opponents shoot worse suppresses opponent efficiency
dTOV Defensive turnover value — forcing turnovers forces turnovers
dSC Defensive second-chance — preventing opponent second chances denies opponent extra shots

net = oTS + oTOV + oSC + dTS + dTOV + dSC. Offense is the first three, defense the last three.

How a "daily re-solve" works

Each day's value is a full RAPM under a 5-year rolling-window time-decay regime: a time-decayed ridge over every possession in the trailing five years, recent games weighted far more via a 550-day half-life (a game's weight is 2^(−age/550)). "As of day D" just means feeding it games dated in (D−5yr, D] — there is no carried-over state, so every day stands alone and the build is causally clean (planting a future game moves the as-of estimate by exactly 0.0). The window is chiefly for speed and cross-league consistency; the 550-day half-life keeps the snapshot fresh (a game ~1.5 years old already weighs half; a 5-year-old game — at the window's edge — 2^(−1825/550) ≈ 0.10, about 10%).

The ridge solves each of the eight shot/decomposition components with its own tuned regularization penalty (a per-component α on offense and defense — the stabilized "LOCK" configuration), plus a small α = 10 group of garbage-time nuisance columns, over a decay-weighted, whitened design. It is a maximum-a-posteriori estimate under a Gaussian prior: the pure series shrinks toward zero; the prior-informed series shrinks each factor toward an informative box+EPM prior inside the ridge (next section).

Scale, by the numbers. The NBA design alone is ~7.1M possession-rows × ~5,800 player-columns. With ~10,200 league-days × five ridge solves each, that's on the order of ~51,000 solves, each over a million-plus possessions — embarrassingly parallel, since every day stands alone.
Net is the sum of the six. The headline net is exactly oTS + oTOV + oSC + dTS + dTOV + dSC (net := sum6) — there is no separate net solve to reconcile, so the closure residual RESID ≡ 0 by construction. The six factors come from four ridge leaf-metrics; offense is the first three, defense the last three.

The Latent view exists only for the ~2,600 players with games in more than one league; for everyone else the single-league board already is the continuous curve, so the toggle simply shows the prior-informed series. On the NBA segment the latent tracks the board at full scale (no compression); across a league jump it carries the translated level through the gap instead of drawing two disjoint lines.

The shrinkage happens inside the ridge — a MAP estimate where each factor's coefficient is pulled toward a prior mean μ rather than toward zero (β ~ N(μ, ·)). Its strength is the ridge penalty itself: strong when a player's decay-weighted exposure is thin (rookies, call-ups, early season) and fading to near-zero as possessions pile up. So prior-informed and pure track each other for established players and on the headline net; the prior mainly rescues thin-data players. Moving the prior into the solve keeps the default series sensible at low data and causal across leagues — its measured, held-out value is a small win-total improvement and a much smoother daily curve, not a transformation of accuracy (see the scorecard).

What the prior is made of. Each factor's prior mean comes from a hyper-sparse linear map of predictive box rates onto that component — three-point value from 3P% and 3PA rate, turnover value from steal rate, second-chance from rebound rates, and so on, twelve component recipes covering all six factors. Since mid-2026 the map is re-fit fresh every game-day on that day's prior-free cross-section (~500 NBA players), so the prior adapts as the league's box-to-impact relationship drifts, instead of being frozen at one historical fit. On a subtle leakage risk: the features are Dunks & Threes' predictive box fields (shot mix, usage, rim rate, steal/block rates), joined leak-safe with source date ≤ as-of date — the map never ingests the EPM impact number itself, which correlates ~0.8 with our own pure net and would make the prior near-circular. The adopted configuration cleared two held-out gates: it improved the frozen-preseason win-total error (MAE 6.94 → 6.80) and made the daily prior-informed series markedly smoother day-to-day (~4× lower median wiggle) at unchanged game-level accuracy. Honest cost: it runs the default board hotter than the pure reference — see the reference match above.

Does it match a known reference?

The pure NBA scale is tuned to reproduce DataBallR's six-factor RAPM (the public time-decay, pure-lineup ridge), anchored at the regular-season end. Across the players the two boards share, the ordering and the full range align tightly — leaders near +7 to +9, the negative tail near −7 — so the model reproduces the reference's shape, not just its top names. DataBallR uses a fixed ~6-year window and this model a 5-year rolling window; because the 550-day half-life discounts old games so steeply, the exact window length barely changes a current rating, while the daily solve still runs one clean curve across a 20-season career.

Two honest caveats. The apples-to-apples comparison is the Pure toggle — the series literally calibrated to the public board. The Explorer default is the prior-informed series, which shrinks each factor toward a daily box+EPM prior and runs hot on the biggest names (up to ~1.5 points on the very top of the board, ~half a point to a point on the rest) — and a touch cool on some defense-first veterans, where the box prior sees less than the lineup data does. And because the daily solve refreshes, exact per-player deltas drift game to game — so rather than pin a decimal-exact comparison table that goes stale, treat the agreement as ordinal-and-range preserving rather than decimal-exact. Flip to Pure on any current star to see the direct match to the reference.

The interesting part: what transfers between leagues?

We don't assume college production means the same thing as NBA production — we measure it, from two-league players (795 G-League → NBA, 1,258 college → NBA). For each factor we fit a possession-weighted affine translation (z_nba = slope·z_feeder + offset): the slope is how much of a skill transfers, and a non-shrinkable variance floor (below) sets how reliably.

Factor NCAA→NBA slope G-League→NBA slope
oTS ? (shooting) 0.160.11

oSC ? (off. 2nd-chance) 0.140.36
dTS (shot defense) 0.240.40
dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.110.71
dSC (def. 2nd-chance) 0.140.24

The model's whole personality is in this table:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely moves your NBA number — a college player who is +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Shooting transfers weakly (G-League slope 0.11, the lowest of the six): efficiency against lower-league defenses says little about scoring on NBA ones.
  • Hustle / instinct carries over best — forcing turnovers (G-League slope 0.71) and offensive second chances (slope 0.36) reflect motor more than skill-against-weaker-competition.
Worked translation. A G-League player grading +1.5 on forcing turnovers (dTOV, the most-transferable factor, slope 0.71) projects to ≈ +1.1 on the NBA scale — but with a wide, non-shrinkable band. The same player at +1.5 in shooting (oTS, slope 0.11) projects to only ≈ +0.17: lower-league scoring almost doesn't carry.

Each translation also carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate): the league jump is genuinely uncertain, so cross-league error bars stay wide and never shrink to zero, however many college games a player has. That honesty is the point — it's why a future MVP's lone college season can sit near replacement level on the NBA axis and still be "consistent" with stardom, as you can see on the Draft board.

Where do these come from? The translations are re-fit whenever the underlying solve regime changes. Under the current per-component "LOCK" regularization the shape is unchanged — every slope still sits well below 1, hustle factors still transfer best — but some magnitudes moved: the NCAA→NBA shooting slope, for instance, came down from ~0.24 to 0.16 when the shot-location components were re-solved with their own penalties. The mover sets that anchor the fits are large and stable (1,258 college→NBA, 795 G-League→NBA), so the affine relationships are robust in direction even as individual slopes are re-estimated. The same mover-anchored machinery also fits the reverse and lateral directions (NCAA→G-League, NBA→G-League), so a player's series can be expressed on any league's scale — about 2,500 two-league careers anchor the network in total. And before any translation is fit, college and G-League possessions are re-scored with an expected-points free-throw adjustment (FT% × value in place of raw makes), which removes free-throw luck from the feeder-league signal; the NBA series is untouched by it.

Confidence intervals

Every factor on every day carries a standard error, se = 100·√σ̂² / √Weff, where Weff is the decay-weighted possession exposure that day. The band widens with few effective possessions (a rookie's first weeks, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up) and narrows as minutes accumulate: at high exposure the net band runs ±1.5 or so; on a thin sample it balloons past ±5. Translated (college / G-League) factors add the non-shrinkable Var_translate floor in quadrature, so their bands stay permanently wide — the league jump is uncertain in a way more games can't fix.

It measures today, not next year. This band is the uncertainty of the current estimate — how tightly the lineup data pins down what a player has already done. It is not a forecast, and read as one it is roughly 4× too wide for a starter and wider still for a rotation player: the measurement se (median ≈ 3.1) dwarfs the median year-over-year move (≈ 0.5) in a series that is already a 700-day low-pass filter. We score that misuse below. The forward band is the amber projection cone on the Explorer — a separately calibrated object.

The forward projection — 1 to 5 years out

Everything above is descriptive — already-measured RAPM. The 2026-27 page and the Explorer's + Projection toggle add the other half: a genuine forward model for "where is this player headed?" It clears the carry-forward bar above, and now runs one to five seasons ahead.

For each of the six factors × each horizon (1yr … 5yr) a separate gradient-boosted model (XGBoost) is trained on 30 seasons of history — from a player's recent trajectory (current value, lags, three-year slope), the box-prior's second opinion, the predictive box stats, age, and role/minutes. Four choices keep it honest:

  • Time-ordered validation — each model trains on the past and is scored strictly on the future, never on data it has seen.
  • A monotonic constraint — a higher current value can never lower the projection, ruling out perverse "great now, so worse later" swings.
  • Survivorship-honest decline — players who leave the league aren't dropped from the target; their factor fades toward replacement level, so the model learns realistic decline and exit instead of assuming everyone ages like the veterans who happened to stick around.
  • A calibrated veteran-decline overlay — measured against its own out-of-fold residuals, the boosted model under-declines aging stars (the per-factor aging drifts nearly cancel in the net). A separate, explicitly fitted per-age annual net correction (kicking in from age 32, re-fit whenever the projection refreshes) is applied on top and re-spread across the six factors so net := sum6 still holds. It is deliberately small — a few tenths per year at the oldest ages — and fully reversible.

It beats plain carry-forward on the factors and horizons measured (dTOV one year out, for instance, at a cross-validated MAE of 0.141, under the carry-forward baseline). The one-year gain is real if modest; the 2-to-5-year horizons are net-new — the old model could only step a single season forward. net is the sum of the six projected factors (never modeled directly), and every projected point carries a band that widens with the horizon: five years out is genuinely more uncertain than one.

See it on the Explorer. Open any current player, flip + Projection on the Net chart, and the blue measured curve grows an orange dashed trajectory out five seasons, a confidence bar at each year. Jokic, for one, is projected to descend from ~+10 today to the mid-5s by 2031 as the age curve bites — the honest arc of a great player past his peak.

Rookie projections come from a separate, deliberately humble model: a prospect's translated college / G-League rating barely predicts rookie-year impact (correlation ≈ 0.08), so rookies regress hard toward a below-replacement baseline. The fit is exposure-weighted — a garbage-time rookie's minutes no longer count as much evidence as a rotation rookie's — which keeps high-pedigree prospects from being dragged flat by end-of-bench noise.

Minutes feed it, but don't inflate it. A companion model projects each player's minutes per game (carry-forward + mean-reversion + an age curve) and allocates each team's 240 minutes across its roster. Those minutes drive availability and team win totals on the 2026-27 page — not the per-100 rating itself, so no one is credited twice for simply playing more.

Limitations & honest negatives

Candor about ceilings and dead ends is part of the product. The short version:

  • The model is near its predictive ceiling. Almost no modeling change moves the out-of-sample numbers; the in-ridge box prior lifts net R² by only a few thousandths, and per-factor gains tend to cancel at the team level. The real unmined levers are tracking data for defense and better availability / age modeling — not another regularization tweak.
  • Defense is the soft spot. Box inputs can't see contested-shot quality, rim protection, or deflections, so the defensive shot / scoring factors carry forward worst (honest single-season corr dTS ≈ 0.30, dSC ≈ 0.27). A tracking-defense spine is built but disabled.
  • Game margins hit a floor. Out-of-sample margin RMSE ≈ 14.4 pts against a ~16.5-pt natural spread sits near the irreducible noise of an NBA game; better ratings barely move it. And this is scored with realized_minutes, so it is rating quality given usage, not a strict pre-game forecast (strict_pregame is not_available).
  • Forward skill is modest, and honestly reported. The headline "net carries forward at r ≈ 0.89" is window-overlap-inflated; the honest, non-overlapping single-season net correlation is ≈ 0.50. On projecting season win totals an external model (Dunks & Threes' EPM) still beats the internal decomposition (win MAE 6.80 vs 6.24).
  • Today's CIs are not forecast bands. They are within-season standard errors; pressed against next season they over-cover (a nominal 50% band catches ≈ 91%). Per-coefficient SEs are an OLS heuristic and understate uncertainty for collinear teammates.
  • Cross-league jumps stay uncertain. Translations are attenuated (variance explained ≈ 1–5% NCAA, ≈ 11% G-League) and fit only on players who reached the NBA, so prospect-board positions are driven largely by the constant — treat draft CIs as very wide by construction.
  • Coverage gaps. NBA play-by-play from 1997–2013 has no steals or blocks (structural zeros); early college seasons (2005–13) run on partial schedules (reduced_sample); and no public play-by-play exists before 1996-97 (NBA) or 2005-06 (college), so the possession-level RAPM cannot extend there.
