Daily RAPM Explorer Model

A daily, multi-league six-factor RAPM — a reader's guide

For someone who already knows the traditional six-factor RAPM decomposition. This explains what's new here, how it works, and what it says — with numbers.

The one-liner

Traditional six-factor RAPM gives you one number per player per season (or per rolling window): the six factors — oTS, oTOV, oREB on offense and dTS, dTOV, dREB on defense, in points per 100 possessions, summing to net. This model gives you that same decomposition re-computed as of every single game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA), all placed on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value carrying a confidence interval.

So instead of "Jokić was +8 in 2025-26," you get Jokić's +8 as a daily curve stretching back through his career, next to a college freshman's daily curve on the same axis, each with error bars.

Three things that are different

1. It's daily, not seasonal. Every day a game was played, the model re-solves the entire ridge regression from scratch, as of that day. A player's rating is a smooth curve, not a step function.

2. It's multi-league and self-consistent. College and G-League players are translated onto the NBA scale, so a sophomore at Duke and an All-NBA center are directly comparable. (More on how, below — it's the most interesting part.)

3. Everything has error bars. Each factor, each day, carries a confidence interval that widens when a player has few possessions (early career, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up) and narrows as the sample grows. Cross-league numbers carry an extra, non-shrinkable uncertainty for the leap between leagues.

A fourth, subtler property matters a lot: the daily curve is smooth, with no between-season jumps. Day-to-day the median change in net is 0.02 points; across an entire offseason it's under 0.8. (An earlier attempt that filtered cheap box-score signals between sparse anchors produced curves that "jumped like crazy" between seasons — this one doesn't, by construction.)

How a "daily re-solve" works — and isn't that an absurd number of solves?

Each day's value is an honest, full RAPM: a time-decayed ridge regression over every possession in the trailing ~6 years, with recent games weighted more (a 700-day half-life — a game from two years ago counts about half as much as one from yesterday). To get "as of day D," you simply feed it the games dated ≤ D. There is no carried-over state — each day stands alone.

Yes, it's a lot of solves. The NBA alone has ~4,750 game-days from 1996-97 to 2025-26; with G-League and college it's ~6,640 league-days, and each one runs five ridge regressions (the four factor sub-models plus a net model) — on the order of 33,000 ridge solves, each over roughly a million-plus possessions and ~6,000 player columns.

The trick is that "no carried-over state" is also a gift: every day is independent, so the whole thing is embarrassingly parallel. What made it tractable on a laptop:

  • Build the design once, reuse it every day. Each league's giant sparse possession matrix is built and cached a single time; a given day just selects the rows dated ≤ D and re-weights them.
  • A 6-year trailing window (which is also the reference's window) keeps each solve to ~1.5M rows instead of all 30 years — about a 10-second solve.
  • Run many days at once, but pin each worker to one CPU thread. The bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not arithmetic; ~12 single-threaded workers saturate it without thrashing (un-pinned, they spawn hundreds of fighting threads and grind to a halt — a lesson learned the hard way).

Net result: the full 30-year NBA daily backfill runs in about an hour.

The interesting part: what actually transfers between leagues?

This is where the multi-league model earns its keep. We don't assume college production means the same thing as NBA production — we measure it, using ~1,000 players who appear in two leagues (786 G-League↔NBA, 250 college→NBA). For each of the six factors we fit how a player's value in the lower league predicts their NBA value. The slope is "how much it transfers"; the correlation R is "how reliably."

Factor NCAA→NBA slope R G-League→NBA slope R
oTS (shooting) 0.14 0.20 0.10 0.08
oTOV (offense, ball security) 0.15 0.09 0.40 0.31
oREB (offensive rebounding) 0.42 0.32 0.39 0.32
dTS (defense, shot defense) 0.43 0.27 0.30 0.20
dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.50 0.31 0.78 0.34
dREB (defensive rebounding) 0.00 −0.01 0.27 0.09

Read this table and the model's whole personality falls out:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely budges your NBA number — the competition gap is enormous. A college player who is +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Shooting transfers the least (R ≈ 0.1–0.2). Being efficient against college defenses says almost nothing about scoring efficiently against NBA defenses.
  • Forcing turnovers and rebounding transfer the most (R ≈ 0.3) — instincts and motor carry over better than skill-against-weaker-competition.
  • Defensive rebounding doesn't transfer at all from college (R = −0.01 — literal zero). Out- rebounding shorter, weaker college players tells you nothing about the NBA, where everyone is elite and box-outs are schemed. The model honestly maps every college player's dREB to the league average. (We let the data decide this rather than hard-coding it; G-League dREB keeps a weak signal, R = 0.09.)

Crucially, the translation also carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate) for every factor — the leap between leagues is genuinely uncertain, so cross-league error bars are wide and never shrink to zero no matter how many college games a player has. That honesty is the point: the model says "here's our best NBA-equivalent guess, and here's how little we really know."

What it looks like for real players

A full career as a smooth curve. LeBron James, net rating sampled across 23 seasons:

Season Net What it captures
2003-04 (rookie) +0.0 replacement-level rookie, very wide CI
2008-09 +6.6 ascending
2012-13 (peak) +6.9 Miami peak
2017-18 +3.1 late-Cleveland decline
2025-26 +1.3 age-41 veteran

No season boundaries, no jumps — just a curve that rises, plateaus, and gently falls, its error band tightening as his minutes pile up and widening again as his role shrinks.

The cross-league reality check. Cooper Flagg, the 2025 No. 1 pick, was an elite college player — his Duke season translates to about +0.4 NBA-equivalent (already heavily discounted by the translation). His actual NBA rookie season started around −2.5 and has been climbing toward −1. The picture tells the true story that a single number hides: dominant college production maps to "promising NBA prospect," and the real NBA learning curve is steep — all on one continuous axis, purple (NCAA) handing off to blue (NBA).

A three-league journey. A two-way player like Jake LaRavia appears in all three colors on one chart — NCAA → NBA with G-League assignments interleaved — every stint on the same NBA-equivalent scale, hovering around replacement level, exactly where a fringe rotation player belongs.

The current NBA leaders, as of 2025-26: Jokić +8.4, Gilgeous-Alexander +7.4, Wembanyama +6.9, Leonard +6.8, Antetokounmpo +6.3 — the expected names, on the expected scale.

That's ~9.8 million player-day rows across ~11,000 players, every one with six factors, a net, and confidence intervals, browsable in the interactive site (site/).

What it doesn't have yet (see DATA_COVERAGE.md): NBA playoffs (it's regular-season only, so ratings freeze in April), and college before 2021-22 (so pre-2021 college careers — like SGA's Kentucky year — don't appear). Those two are the highest-value things to collect next.
