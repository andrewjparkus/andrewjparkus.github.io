Daily RAPM Explorer Model

A daily, multi-league six-factor RAPM — a reader's guide

For someone who already knows the traditional six-factor RAPM decomposition. This explains what's new here, how it works, and what it says — with numbers.

The one-liner

Traditional six-factor RAPM gives you one number per player per season (or per rolling window): the six factors — oTS, oTOV, oSC on offense and dTS, dTOV, dSC on defense, in points per 100 possessions, summing to net. This model gives you that same decomposition re-computed as of every single game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA), all placed on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value carrying a confidence interval.

So instead of "Jokić was +8 in 2025-26," you get Jokić's +8 as a daily curve stretching back through his career and through the playoffs, next to a college freshman's daily curve on the same axis, each with error bars.

A note on the sixth factor. What older boards labeled "oREB/dREB" is, in this decomposition, second-chance value (oSC/dSC): the points a player adds after the offense's own missed shot — putbacks, kickouts off the offensive glass, and on defense the prevention of those same second chances. It is not a rebound rate. We label it oSC/dSC to match its actual construction (the RAPM DECOMP definition), where e.g. Jokić grades dSC +2.0 and Wembanyama dSC +1.1.

Three things that are different

1. It's daily, not seasonal. Every day a game was played, the model re-solves the entire ridge regression from scratch, as of that day. A player's rating is a smooth curve, not a step function. The NBA series runs regular season and playoffs, so a star's curve flows continuously from April into June with no break (Jokić's net moves +7.98 on the last regular-season day to +7.98 on the first playoff day — zero discontinuity, because each day only sees games dated ≤ that day).

2. It's multi-league and self-consistent. College and G-League players are translated onto the NBA scale, so a sophomore at Duke and an All-NBA center are directly comparable. (More on how, below — it's the most interesting part.)

3. Everything has error bars. Each factor, each day, carries a confidence interval that widens when a player has few possessions (early career, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up) and narrows as the sample grows. Cross-league numbers carry an extra, non-shrinkable uncertainty for the leap between leagues.

A fourth, subtler property matters a lot: the daily curve is smooth, with no between-season jumps. Day-to-day the median change in net is 0.02 points; across an entire offseason it's under 0.8. (An earlier attempt that filtered cheap box-score signals between sparse anchors produced curves that "jumped like crazy" between seasons — this one doesn't, by construction.)

How a "daily re-solve" works — and isn't that an absurd number of solves?

Each day's value is an honest, full RAPM: a time-decayed ridge regression over every possession in the trailing ~6 years, with recent games weighted more (a 700-day half-life — a game from two years ago counts about half as much as one from yesterday). To get "as of day D," you simply feed it the games dated ≤ D. There is no carried-over state — each day stands alone.

Yes, it's a lot of solves. The NBA alone has ~6,080 game-days from 1996-97 to 2025-26 (regular season and playoffs); with G-League and college it's ~10,200 league-days, and each one runs five ridge regressions (the four factor sub-models plus a net model) — on the order of 51,000 ridge solves, each over roughly a million-plus possessions and ~6,000 player columns.

The trick is that "no carried-over state" is also a gift: every day is independent, so the whole thing is embarrassingly parallel. What made it tractable on a laptop:

  • Build the design once, reuse it every day. Each league's giant sparse possession matrix is built and cached a single time (the NBA design alone is ~7.1M possession-rows; the 21-season college design ~14.7M); a given day just selects the rows dated ≤ D and re-weights them.
  • A 6-year trailing window (which is also the reference's window) keeps each solve to ~1.5M rows instead of all 30 years — about a 10-second solve.
  • Run many days at once, but pin each worker to one CPU thread. The bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not arithmetic; single-threaded workers saturate it without thrashing (un-pinned, they spawn hundreds of fighting threads and grind to a halt — a lesson learned the hard way). How many workers fit is set by RAM, since each holds a multi-GB design resident: on a 26 GB laptop that's ~3 NBA workers and 1–2 college workers, scaling up with memory.

Does it match a known reference?

The NBA scale is tuned to reproduce DataBallR's six-factor RAPM (the public 6-year, time-decay, pure-lineup ridge). The tuning is anchored at the end of the regular season (DataBallR is regular-season only); by causality that as-of solve is playoff-free, so the comparison stays clean even though our series continues into the playoffs. The interesting part: what actually transfers between leagues?

This is where the multi-league model earns its keep. We don't assume college production means the same thing as NBA production — we measure it, using ~1,570 players who appear in two leagues (786 G-League↔NBA, 788 college→NBA). For each of the six factors we fit how a player's value in the lower league predicts their NBA value. The slope is "how much it transfers"; the correlation R is "how reliably."

Factor NCAA→NBA slope R G-League→NBA slope R
oTS (shooting) 0.26 0.31 0.10 0.11
oTOV (offense, ball security) 0.17 0.22 0.41 0.38
oSC (offensive second-chance) 0.22 0.22 0.40 0.32
dTS (defense, shot defense) 0.26 0.22 0.31 0.20
dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.15 0.14 0.82 0.47
dSC (defensive second-chance) 0.14 0.11 0.28 0.20

Read this table and the model's whole personality falls out:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely budges your NBA number — the competition gap is enormous. A college player who is +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Shooting transfers weakly from the G-League (R ≈ 0.1) and only modestly from college. Being efficient against lower-league defenses says little about scoring against NBA defenses.
  • The hustle/instinct channels carry over best — forcing turnovers (G-League R 0.47) and generating offensive second chances (oSC R ≈ 0.3) reflect motor more than skill-against-weaker- competition, so they survive the jump better.
  • Defensive second-chance barely transfers from college (dSC R = 0.11). Out-working shorter, weaker college bigs on the defensive glass tells you little about the NBA, where everyone is elite and box-outs are schemed. (We let the data decide this rather than hard-coding it.)

Crucially, the translation also carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate) for every factor — the leap between leagues is genuinely uncertain, so cross-league error bars are wide and never shrink to zero no matter how many college games a player has. That honesty is the point: the model says "here's our best NBA-equivalent guess, and here's how little we really know." (It is why, e.g., a future MVP's lone college season can sit near replacement level on the NBA axis and still be "consistent" with stardom — the band is that wide.)

What it looks like for real players

A full career as a smooth curve. LeBron James, net rating across 23 seasons, rises from a replacement-level rookie (very wide CI) through a Miami-era peak near +7, then a gentle age-41 decline toward +1 — no season boundaries, no jumps, the error band tightening as his minutes pile up and widening again as his role shrinks.

The cross-league reality check. Cooper Flagg, the 2025 No. 1 pick, was an elite college player — his Duke season translates to about +0.2 NBA-equivalent (already heavily discounted by the translation). His actual NBA rookie season has hovered around −1. The picture tells the true story a single number hides: dominant college production maps to "promising NBA prospect," and the real NBA learning curve is steep — all on one continuous axis, purple (NCAA) handing off to blue (NBA). And because college now reaches back to 2005-06, sequential college→NBA careers that span the pre-2021 era (an SGA-at-Kentucky type) appear in full.

A three-league journey. A two-way player like Jake LaRavia appears in all three colors on one chart — NCAA → NBA with G-League assignments interleaved — every stint on the same NBA-equivalent scale, hovering around replacement level, exactly where a fringe rotation player belongs.

The current NBA leaders, as of 2025-26: Jokić +8.0, Gilgeous-Alexander +7.4, Wembanyama +6.9, Leonard +6.7, Antetokounmpo +6.3 — the expected names, on the expected scale.

The system at a glance

Coverage Players Game-days

College 2005-06 → 2025-26 42,378 2,953

That's ~42 million player-day rows, every one with six factors, a net, and confidence intervals, browsable in the interactive site. College seasons 2005-06 through 2012-13 have thinner historical play-by-play coverage (21–67% of scheduled games) and are flagged reduced_sample so they can be down-weighted rather than trusted blindly; coverage is full (≥85%) from 2013-14 on.

What's next (lower-value, not yet in): NBA pre-1996 and pre-2005 college exist only as box-score priors (no public play-by-play), so the possession-level RAPM can't extend there — those eras are covered approximately by box-only strength priors rather than the daily series.
