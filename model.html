The one-liner

Classic six-factor RAPM gives one number per player per season: six components (points per 100 possessions, summing to net), offense and defense. This model re-computes that decomposition as of every game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA) on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value with a confidence interval.

So not "Jokic was +8 in 2025-26" but a daily curve across his career, playoffs included, beside a college freshman's on the same axis. Reading the reference (pure) DataBallR scale: net +5 is clear All-Star, the league centers near 0, the best sit +7 to +9 (one MVP-tier outlier above), the negative tail near −7. The default prior-informed series runs hotter — see the reference match.

The sixth factor, renamed. What older boards called "oREB/dREB" is here second-chance value (oSC/dSC): points added after the offense's own miss — putbacks and kickouts off the offensive glass; on defense, preventing them. Not a rebound rate (Jokic grades dSC ≈ +2.6, Wembanyama ≈ +1.1).

The six factors

Three offensive, three defensive, each in points per 100 possessions on the NBA-equivalent scale, sign-aligned so positive is always good. The decomposition is not ours: it follows Russell Thomas's additive RAPM decomposition, and the ridge solver core is a vendored, byte-identical copy of his reference implementation. This project is the daily, multi-league product layer on top.

FactorWhat it measuresPositive means
oTS Offensive true-shooting value (EFG + FT value) scores efficiently
oTOV Offensive turnover value — ball security protects the ball
oSC Offensive second-chance value creates extra possessions
dTS Defensive true-shooting — making opponents shoot worse suppresses opponent efficiency
dTOV Defensive turnover value — forcing turnovers forces turnovers
dSC Defensive second-chance — preventing opponent second chances denies opponent extra shots

net = oTS + oTOV + oSC + dTS + dTOV + dSC — offense the first three, defense the last three. Net is exactly that sum (net := sum6), never a separate solve to reconcile, so the closure residual RESID ≡ 0 by construction. The six come from four ridge leaf-metrics.

How a "daily re-solve" works

Each day's value is a full RAPM under a 5-year rolling-window time-decay regime: a ridge over every possession in the trailing five years, weighted 2^(−age/550) — a 550-day half-life. "As of day D" means games dated in (D−5yr, D]. There is no carried-over state: every day stands alone, and planting a future game moves the as-of estimate by exactly 0.0. The window buys speed and cross-league consistency; the half-life keeps the snapshot fresh (a game ~1.5 years old already weighs half; at the window's edge, 2^(−1825/550) ≈ 0.10).

The ridge solves each of the eight shot/decomposition components with its own tuned penalty (a per-component α on offense and defense — the stabilized "LOCK" configuration), plus a small α = 10 group of garbage-time nuisance columns, over a decay-weighted, whitened design.

Scale, by the numbers. The NBA design alone is ~7.1M possession-rows × ~5,800 player-columns. At ~10,200 league-days × five ridge solves each, that's ~51,000 solves, each over a million-plus possessions — embarrassingly parallel, since every day stands alone.

The Explorer's mode toggle switches every chart between four daily series. The first three share dates, leagues and error bars, differing only in what each factor shrinks toward.

Latent One continuous curve for league-movers: a DARKO-style state-space pass (per-factor Kalman + smoother) over the daily prior-informed board, with college/G-League observations affine-mapped to the NBA scale. Display-only — it never feeds the board or the projection. — (a re-drawing of the prior-informed board, not a new solve)

Latent exists only for the ~2,600 players with games in more than one league; for everyone else the single-league board already is the continuous curve. On the NBA segment it tracks the board at full scale; across a league jump it carries the translated level through the gap, removing the seam.

Shrinkage happens inside the ridge — a maximum-a-posteriori estimate pulling each coefficient toward a prior mean μ rather than zero (β ~ N(μ, ·)), at a strength set by the ridge penalty: strong when decay-weighted exposure is thin, fading to near-zero as possessions pile up. So prior-informed and pure track each other for established players; the prior mainly rescues thin-data ones — a rookie or call-up starts somewhere sensible — without transforming accuracy (see the scorecard).

What the prior is made of. Each factor's prior mean is a hyper-sparse linear map of predictive box rates onto that component (three-point value from 3P% and 3PA rate, turnover value from steal rate, and so on) — twelve recipes across the six factors. Since mid-2026 it is re-fit fresh every game-day on that day's prior-free cross-section (~500 NBA players), so it adapts as the league's box-to-impact relationship drifts. Leakage is guarded: the features are Dunks & Threes' predictive box fields, joined with source date ≤ as-of date, and the map never ingests the EPM impact number itself — it correlates ~0.8 with our own pure net and would make the prior near-circular. It cleared two held-out gates: it lowered frozen-preseason win-total error (current figures above) at a ~4× lower median daily wiggle and unchanged game-level accuracy. Honest cost: it runs the default board hotter than the pure reference.

Does it match a known reference?

The pure NBA scale is tuned to reproduce DataBallR's six-factor RAPM (the public time-decay, pure-lineup ridge), anchored at the regular-season end. Across shared players the ordering and the full range align tightly — leaders near +7 to +9, the negative tail near −7 — so the shape reproduces, not just the top names. DataBallR uses a fixed ~6-year window, this model a 5-year rolling window; the 550-day half-life discounts old games so steeply that window length barely changes a current rating, while the daily solve still runs one clean curve across a 20-season career.

Two honest caveats. First, the apples-to-apples comparison is the Pure toggle — the series literally calibrated to the public board. The Explorer default is prior-informed, which runs hot on the biggest names (up to ~1.5 points at the very top, ~half a point to a point on the rest) and a touch cool on some defense-first veterans, where the box prior sees less than the lineup data does. Second, the daily solve refreshes, so exact per-player deltas drift game to game: the agreement is ordinal-and-range preserving, not decimal-exact. Flip to Pure on any current star to see the direct match.

The interesting part: what transfers between leagues?

We don't assume college production means what NBA production means — we measure it on two-league players (795 G-League → NBA, 1,258 college → NBA). Each factor gets a possession-weighted affine translation (z_nba = slope·z_feeder + offset): the slope is how much of a skill transfers, a non-shrinkable variance floor (below) how reliably.

Factor NCAA→NBA slope G-League→NBA slope
oTS ? (shooting) 0.160.11

oSC ? (off. 2nd-chance) 0.140.36

dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.110.71
dSC (def. 2nd-chance) 0.140.24

The model's personality is in this table:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely moves your NBA number: a college player at +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Out of the G-League, motor travels and shooting doesn't. Forcing turnovers carries furthest (slope 0.71) and shooting least (0.11) — motor rather than dominance over weaker competition. Out of college every slope is low enough that the ordering barely matters.
Worked translation. A G-League player grading +1.5 on forcing turnovers (dTOV, the most-transferable factor out of that league, slope 0.71) projects to ≈ +1.1 on the NBA scale — with a wide, non-shrinkable band. At +1.5 in shooting (oTS, slope 0.11) he projects to only ≈ +0.17: lower-league scoring almost doesn't carry.

Each translation carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate): cross-league error bars stay wide and never shrink to zero, however many college games a player has. That's why a future MVP's lone college season can sit near replacement level and still be "consistent" with stardom — see the Draft board.

Where do these come from? They are re-fit whenever the solve regime changes. Under the current per-component "LOCK" regularization the shape held, but magnitudes moved: the NCAA→NBA shooting slope came down from ~0.24 to 0.16 when the shot-location components were re-solved with their own penalties. The anchoring mover sets are large and stable (1,258 college→NBA, 795 G-League→NBA), so the relationships are robust in direction even as individual slopes are re-estimated. The same machinery fits the reverse and lateral directions (NCAA→G-League, NBA→G-League) — about 2,500 two-league careers anchor the network. Before any translation is fit, college and G-League possessions are re-scored with an expected-points free-throw adjustment (FT% × value in place of raw makes), removing free-throw luck from the feeder signal; the NBA series is untouched.

Confidence intervals

Every factor on every day carries a standard error, se = 100·√σ̂² / √Weff, where Weff is that day's decay-weighted possession exposure. The band widens with few effective possessions and narrows as minutes accumulate: at high exposure the net band runs ±1.5 or so; on a thin sample it balloons past ±5. Translated (college / G-League) factors add the non-shrinkable Var_translate floor in quadrature, so their bands stay permanently wide.

It measures today, not next year. The band says how tightly the lineup data pins down what a player has already done; read as a next-season forecast it is the wrong object, roughly 4× too wide for a starter and wider still for a rotation player — the measurement se (median ≈ 3.1) dwarfs the median year-over-year move (≈ 0.5) in a series that is already a 550-day low-pass filter. We score that misuse below. The forward band is the amber projection cone on the Explorer, separately calibrated. Per-coefficient se is an OLS heuristic and understates uncertainty for collinear teammates.

Frozen forecast scoreboard — tamper-evident accuracy

Every accuracy number on this page is re-derived from a frozen on-disk artifact, and every frozen forecast is re-checked against the source file it names. If a figure no longer reproduces within ±0.01, or a forecast no longer matches its source, this panel refuses to render rather than show a stale number.

What makes this falsifiable: season-ahead forecasts (2026-27 win totals and playoff odds) live in scoreboard.json, and every vintage of that file is committed to this site's public git repo, timestamped, before the season tips. That history is the whole of the tamper-evidence — you can read what we predicted and when, and the re-freezes are visible in it: OKC ran 63.6 → 66.0 → 66.1 → 64.6 across four public vintages as the roster and depth chart were corrected. What is not evidence: the chain_ok flag. It checks an internal build ledger that is never published and is rebuilt from scratch at each freeze, so it verifies a file we just wrote and cannot tell you a forecast was never re-frozen. Only the git history can.

The forward projection — 1 to 5 years out

The 2026-27 page and the Explorer's + Projection toggle add the other half: a genuine forward model for "where is this player headed?"

Each of the six factors × each horizon (1yr … 5yr) gets its own gradient-boosted model (XGBoost), trained on 30 seasons of history from a player's recent trajectory (current value, lags, three-year slope), the box-prior's second opinion, the predictive box stats, age and role/minutes. Four choices keep it honest:

  • Time-ordered validation — trains on the past, scored strictly on the future.
  • A monotonic constraint — a higher current value can never lower the projection, ruling out perverse "great now, so worse later" swings.
  • Survivorship-honest decline — players who leave the league aren't dropped from the target; their factor fades toward replacement level, so the model learns real decline instead of assuming everyone ages like the veterans who stuck around.
  • A calibrated veteran-decline overlay — against its own out-of-fold residuals the boosted model under-declines aging stars (the per-factor aging drifts nearly cancel in the net). An explicitly fitted per-age annual net correction (from age 32, re-fit whenever the projection refreshes) is applied on top and re-spread across the six factors so net := sum6 still holds. Deliberately small — a few tenths per year at the oldest ages — and fully reversible.

It beats plain carry-forward on the factors and horizons measured (dTOV one year out, at a cross-validated MAE of 0.141). The one-year gain is real if modest; the 2-to-5-year horizons are net-new. net is the sum of the six projected factors, never modeled directly, and every projected point carries a band that widens with the horizon.

See it on the Explorer. Flip + Projection on any player's Net chart: the measured curve grows an orange dashed trajectory out five seasons, a confidence bar at each year. Jokic descends from ~+10 today to the mid-5s by 2031 as the age curve bites — the honest arc of a great player past his peak.

Rookies get a separate, deliberately humble model: a prospect's translated college / G-League rating barely predicts rookie-year impact (correlation ≈ 0.08), so rookies regress hard toward a below-replacement baseline. The fit is exposure-weighted — a garbage-time rookie's minutes count as less evidence than a rotation rookie's — which keeps high-pedigree prospects from being dragged flat by end-of-bench noise.

Minutes feed it, but don't inflate it. A companion model projects each player's minutes per game (carry-forward + mean-reversion + an age curve) and allocates each team's 240 minutes across its roster. Those minutes drive availability and team win totals — not the per-100 rating, so no one is credited twice for playing more.

Limitations & honest negatives

  • Near its predictive ceiling. Margin RMSE ≈ 14.4 pts against a ~16.5-pt natural spread is close to an NBA game's irreducible noise, and almost no modeling change moves it. Margins are scored with realized_minutes — rating quality given usage, not a strict pre-game forecast (strict_pregame is not_available). The unmined levers are tracking data for defense and age / availability modeling, not another regularization tweak.
  • Defense is the soft spot. Box inputs can't see contested-shot quality, rim protection or deflections, so the defensive factors carry forward worst (honest single-season corr dTS ≈ 0.30, dSC ≈ 0.27). A tracking-defense spine is built but disabled.
  • Forward skill trails EPM. On season win totals Dunks & Threes' EPM still beats the internal decomposition (figures above).
  • Cross-league jumps stay uncertain. Translations are attenuated (variance explained ≈ 1–5% NCAA, ≈ 11% G-League) and fit only on players who reached the NBA, so draft CIs are very wide by construction.
  • Coverage gaps. NBA play-by-play carries no steals or blocks for 1997–2013 (structural zeros); early college seasons (2005–13) run on partial schedules (reduced_sample); no public play-by-play exists before 1996-97 (NBA) or 2005-06 (college).
