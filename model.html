A daily, multi-league six-factor RAPM — a reader's guide

For readers who know the traditional six-factor RAPM decomposition: what's new here, how it works, and what it says — with numbers.

The one-liner

Traditional six-factor RAPM gives one number per player per season: the six factors — oTS, oTOV, oSC on offense, dTS, dTOV, dSC on defense (points per 100, summing to net). This model re-computes that decomposition as of every game-day, for three leagues at once (NBA, G-League, NCAA) on one NBA-equivalent scale, each value with a confidence interval.

So instead of "Jokić was +8 in 2025-26," you get his rating as a daily curve across his career and through the playoffs, beside a college freshman's curve on the same axis — each with error bars.

The sixth factor. What older boards called "oREB/dREB" is here second-chance value (oSC/dSC): points added after the offense's own miss — putbacks, kickouts off the offensive glass, and on defense the prevention of those second chances. It is not a rebound rate (Jokić grades dSC +2.0, Wembanyama +1.1).

Four things that are different

Daily, not seasonal. Every game-day, the model re-solves the full ridge regression as of that day — a smooth curve, not a step function. The NBA series spans regular season and playoffs with no break: each day sees only games dated ≤ itself, so a star's net flows continuously from April into June.

Multi-league and self-consistent. College and G-League players are translated onto the NBA scale, so a Duke sophomore and an All-NBA center are directly comparable (more below — it's the most interesting part).

Error bars on everything. Each factor, each day, carries a confidence interval that widens with few possessions (early career, a cup of coffee, a recent call-up) and narrows as the sample grows. Cross-league numbers carry an extra, non-shrinkable uncertainty for the leap between leagues.

Smooth, no between-season jumps. Day-to-day the median change in net is 0.02 points; across a full offseason, under 0.8 — by construction, not smoothing.

How a "daily re-solve" works

Each day's value is a full RAPM: a time-decayed ridge over every possession in the trailing ~6 years, recent games weighted more (a 700-day half-life). "As of day D" just means feeding it games dated ≤ D — there is no carried-over state, so every day stands alone.

That independence is a gift: the whole thing is embarrassingly parallel, and each league's giant possession matrix is built once and re-sliced per day. The NBA alone has ~6,080 game-days; with the other leagues it's ~10,200 league-days × five ridge solves each — on the order of 51,000 solves, each over a million-plus possessions.

The box prior: sharpening low-data ratings

Pure RAPM shrinks a thin sample toward zero, so a rookie or fresh call-up sits near replacement level until the possessions accumulate. Here, three of the six factors instead shrink toward an informative box-score prior — a per-player skill estimate built from predictive box rates. With little on-court data the rating leans on that estimate; as possessions pile up, the lineup data takes over.

The prior ships only where it provably helps. Each factor's prior was tuned and tested out-of-sample — does an end-of-season rating predict next season better? — and kept only if it lowered that error without hurting the established stars or the rest of the decomposition. Three passed: shooting (oTS), ball-security (oTOV), and forcing turnovers (dTOV). The other three — second-chance value (oSC/dSC) and shot defense (dTS) — shrink to zero as before; their box priors didn't generalize forward, so we don't use them. The prior-free series stays the canonical, DataBallR-matched reference; the curves shown here are the prior-informed version.

The interesting part: what transfers between leagues?

Here the multi-league model earns its keep. We don't assume college production means the same thing as NBA production — we measure it, using ~1,570 two-league players (786 G-League↔NBA, 788 college→NBA). For each factor we fit how lower-league value predicts NBA value: the slope is how much transfers, the correlation R how reliably.

Factor NCAA→NBA slope R G-League→NBA slope R
oTS (shooting) 0.26 0.31 0.10 0.11
oTOV (ball security) 0.17 0.22 0.41 0.38
oSC (offensive 2nd-chance) 0.22 0.22 0.40 0.32
dTS (shot defense) 0.26 0.22 0.31 0.20
dTOV (forcing turnovers) 0.15 0.14 0.82 0.47
dSC (defensive 2nd-chance) 0.14 0.11 0.28 0.20

The model's whole personality is in this table:

  • Every slope is well below 1. Dominating a lower league barely moves your NBA number — the competition gap is enormous. A college player who is +5 in true shooting projects to roughly average NBA shooting.
  • Shooting transfers weakly (G-League R ≈ 0.1, college only modestly): efficiency against lower-league defenses says little about scoring on NBA ones.
  • Hustle/instinct carries over best — forcing turnovers (G-League R 0.47) and generating offensive second chances (oSC R ≈ 0.3) reflect motor more than skill-against-weaker-competition.
  • Defensive second-chance barely transfers from college (dSC R = 0.11): out-working weaker college bigs on the glass tells you little about the NBA. We let the data decide this.

The translation also carries an irreducible uncertainty floor (Var_translate) per factor — the league jump is genuinely uncertain, so cross-league error bars are wide and never shrink to zero, however many college games a player has. That honesty is the point: it's why a future MVP's lone college season can sit near replacement level on the NBA axis and still be "consistent" with stardom — the band is that wide.

What it looks like for real players

A full career as a smooth curve. LeBron James across 23 seasons rises from a replacement-level rookie (wide CI) through a Miami-era peak, then a gentle age-41 decline — no season boundaries, no jumps, the error band tightening as his minutes pile up and widening as his role shrinks.

The cross-league reality check. Cooper Flagg, the 2025 No. 1 pick, was elite in college — yet his Duke season translates to roughly replacement level (about 0.0 NBA-equivalent, heavily discounted), and his actual NBA rookie year has hovered near −1.3. The picture tells the story a single number hides: dominant college production maps to "promising prospect," and the real NBA learning curve is steep — all on one axis, NCAA handing off to NBA. Because college now reaches back to 2005-06, pre-2021 college→NBA careers (an SGA-at-Kentucky type) appear in full.

A three-league journey. A two-way player like Jake LaRavia shows all three colors on one chart — NCAA → NBA with G-League stints interleaved — every leg on the same scale, hovering near replacement level, exactly where a fringe rotation player belongs.

That's ~42 million player-day rows, each with six factors, a net, and confidence intervals. College seasons 2005-06 through 2012-13 have thinner play-by-play coverage (21–67% of scheduled games) and are flagged reduced_sample to be down-weighted, not trusted blindly; coverage is full (≥85%) from 2013-14 on.

What's next (lower-value): NBA pre-1996 and pre-2005 college have no public play-by-play, so the possession-level RAPM can't extend there — those eras would need box-only strength priors rather than the daily series.
