Twenty-four seasons of game margins uRAPM vs EPM vs DARKO, at equal information

Every source aggregated with the same minutes, scaled by the same fitted slope, scored on the same games — 24,003 of them, 2003–2026. The comparison our published Task A row could not make.

Why re-run it. The published Task A number gives our rating the scored game's realized minutes and gives EPM and DARKO pre-game shares. That is not a model comparison, it is a minutes head start. Here every arm is aggregated identically, every arm's scale is fit identically, and a game is scored only if every arm has a rating for it — so within a season all three share one denominator.

What equal information does to the published lead

On the published window (2024–2026, n = 3,237) our lead over EPM was 0.1964 points of RMSE (14.079 vs 14.276). Almost all of it is a head start rather than a result — but which head start matters, and there is more than one. The move from the published pair to a parity re-run mixes three separate changes, so the page separates them instead of quoting the total.

The equal-information answer is stage 2: -0.0235 — inside noise, not the published -0.1964. Getting there costs the published lead +0.1987 for the minutes (stage 1: EPM re-aggregated on the same realized minutes our number always used) and a further -0.0258 for the shared scale fit, the common scored set and the source-neutral replacement rule (stage 2).

Why stage 3 is on this page and must not be quoted as the answer.

Every other number here is fitted on all 24 seasons — one pooled slope across the whole history. Refit that way, the same 3,237 games give 14.054 vs 14.109, a gap of -0.0546. That is not what information parity buys: the extra -0.0311 is the training pool, and it is a penalty that lands almost entirely on the external sources — a single slope stretched over 24 seasons cannot absorb their era miscalibration, while ours is refit against it. Between stage 2 and stage 3, EPM moves +0.0315 and DARKO +0.0696, while uRAPM moves +0.0004. 57% of the stage-3 gap is the pool change, not information parity. Reporting -0.0546 as “the published window on equal information” would credit us with a training-pool artifact.
A consistency check on stage 2 — and what it is not. The parity EPM figure above, 14.077146, is the same quantity epm_anchor.py reports as epm_team_realized_minutes_LEAKY_parallel_to_L1: 14.077139. They agree to four decimal places (|Δ| = 0.000007), and the exporter re-reads that value from out/p2_0/epm_anchor_report.json on every build rather than trusting the artifact's copy of it — if it drifts, this page refuses to render. These are not two independent implementations. This module calls epm_anchor.py's own functions, and the two figures share their cache reader, minutes normalisation, walk-forward folds and scale fit by design. So the agreement shows that the parity aggregation on this side reproduces the anchor's — nothing dropped, double-counted or misweighted between the two call sites — and it cannot catch an error in the code they share, which is most of the pipeline. Worth having, and worth not overselling.

Caveat 1 — RMSE is not era-comparable. Compare down a column, never across.

Realized game-margin SD rises from 12.51 (2003) to 16.47 (2026), with a low of 11.98 (2004) and a high of 16.47 (2026). Teams finish further apart than they used to, so an identical model scores several points “better” in the early era on raw RMSE alone. rmse / SD is the cross-era read, which is why it is the large figure in every cell and the raw RMSE is the small one underneath. Read 2026 against 2004 to see why: uRAPM's raw RMSE is 14.124 in 2026 versus 10.972 in 2004 — 3.152 points “worse” — while its scale-free ratio is 0.857 versus 0.916, i.e. better. Margin SD went 11.98 → 16.47 in between; the raw column is reading the league, not the model.

Season by season

One row per season. The large number is rmse / margin SD (scale-free, comparable across eras); the small number under it is raw RMSE in points. n is the commonly-scored game count, not the season's full schedule — a game counts only if every arm can rate it.

The tie band. Where the top two arms are within ±0.05 points of RMSE, the season is called tied and nobody is bolded. That is the same noise band the metric leaderboard judges its game anchors against, and the verdict is derived from the gap, never eyeballed. Banding on points rather than on the ratio is deliberate — 0.05 of a ~0.9 ratio would tie every season — and costs nothing, because within a season the two orderings are identical by construction (shared game set → shared SD denominator).

Caveat 2 — the deep seasons run our model outside the era it was tuned on.

The live configuration (LOCK alphas, HL550) was tuned on 2021-2026 — out-of-time prediction of scoring margin on NBA possessions from that window, and nothing older. Everything left of it on this page is out-of-tune territory: a fair test of the method, an unfair test of this fit.

The −9.6 figure, and what it does and does not say about us. A related configuration was measured at −9.6 on 2015–19. That number is cited, not reproduced here, and it is not ours: it is Russell Thomas's v2 joint-alpha configuration, and what it measures is that config's PREDICTIVE score on the 2015-19 window (a fold-A sibling scored +60.6 on the same build; the v2 config went 2/6 cuts positive) — an accuracy result, from alt3_3factor_research/stabilized_rapm/docs/STABILIZED_RAPM.md and NOTE_FOR_ANDREW_ALPHA_TRANSFER.md.

Our own record contains a deep-era check that passed (G3 era-transfer: new-vs-old board net correlation 0.987 on 2015-19, from daily_v5/out/stab/stab_gate.md) — but it answers a different question. It measures STABILITY: how far the config change moved our own 2015-19 ratings. It does NOT score those ratings against 2015-19 margins. So the caution is neither confirmed nor cleared for this board. Russell Thomas measured whether that config predicted 2015–19; we measured whether ours moved on 2015–19. A board can be perfectly stable under a config change and still predict that era badly, so passing ours does not answer his. The deep columns on this page are, as far as we know, the first accuracy read on the question — which is why they are published with the spine and freshness caveats attached rather than as a verdict.

The artifact says the same thing without any appeal to that history: the paired season-blocked bootstrap of RMSE(uRAPM) − RMSE(EPM) is +0.1277 over 2003–2014 (95% CI [+0.0817, +0.1788], entirely above zero — EPM ahead) and -0.0146 over 2021–2026 (CI [-0.0572, +0.0249], spanning zero). The sign flips at roughly the boundary of the window the config was tuned on.

Caveat 3 — our EPM-independent board loses to EPM. That is the finding.

Pooled over all 24,003 games, uRAPM pure scores 12.477 against EPM's 12.362 — 0.1151 behind it, and it wins 2 of 24 seasons outright (the headline hybrid arm wins 5). On the published window itself — the one the parity table above reconciles — pure scores 14.120 against EPM's 14.077, so it loses the headline window too, where the hybrid arm (14.054) is tied with it — 0.0235, inside the ±0.05 band this page calls a tie everywhere else. The paired bootstrap above puts the full-window difference at +0.1151 (95% CI [+0.0827, +0.1498]). Read this column, not the headline one, for the question “is our model better than EPM”; the headline arm's answer is partly EPM's own.

Caveat 4 — minutes are matched exactly; rating age is not, and the mismatch favours us.

Every arm reads its value as-of ≤ D−1, so no arm sees the future. But the arms are not equally fresh. Our daily solve re-estimates every player on every league date, so the board's value is at most one game-date old. EPM and DARKO publish a row only on a player's own game dates, so a carried-forward value is typically several days old. Minute-weighted, that is:

Caveat 5 — the deep seasons are an incomplete and team-unbalanced sample.

The games spine is not the full schedule before 2014. In 2007 it carries 1,037 games with teams playing between 57 and 78 apiece — at most 89% of that season, and not a random sample of it. Games-per-team spread runs above 2 in 2003–2013 , and again in 2020.

What this does not touch: the head-to-head. Every arm is scored on the identical game set, so a missing game is missing for all of them and the comparison stays fair. What it does touch: every era-level absolute on this page — margin SD, raw RMSE, the shape of the rolling chart — and the claim that a row here is that NBA season. It is not; it is what our spine has of it. The completeness figure is measured against each season's own best-covered team, so it is a floor on the shortfall, never a ceiling.

Caveat 6 — a slice of every team rating is imputed, and the tail is not tiny.

A player with no as-of value in a source is filled at that source's replacement level, so covered minute share is the fraction of a team-game the numbers actually saw. The season means are 0.990 and up for every source in every season, which is why a guard on the mean is worthless — it cannot fire. The tail is the real story: in 2020 the headline arm falls below 0.95 coverage on 149 team-games and below 0.90 on 48, with a worst single team-game of 0.6930.

The guard this build enforces is on those tail statistics, not on the mean, and each threshold is published beside the worst value observed so the headroom is checkable: mean floor 0.8 vs observed 0.9903; min floor 0.4 vs observed 0.4995; frac_below_0_90 ceiling 0.05 vs observed 0.0257; frac_below_0_95 ceiling 0.12 vs observed 0.0705. At a floor of 0.50 the worst-team-game guard fired on a single 2005 game at 0.4995; all four sources report that identical value there, so it is a player none of them can rate rather than a gap one arm has, and the floor sits below it deliberately.

Caveat 7 — deep-season vintage symmetry is assumed, not verified. Open question.

Every arm here is scored as-of ≤ D−1, but that only controls when a value was read, not which model produced it. Whether Dunks & Threes' and DARKO's historical APIs serve values as they were published then or values retro-scored by today's model is an open question we have not resolved. If either serves retro-scored history, its deep-season figures carry an advantage ours does not, and the deep columns are not vintage-symmetric. We are stating the assumption rather than quietly relying on it.

Method & what this is not

  • Identical aggregation. 5.0 * sum_i (m_i / M) * v_s(player_i, as-of D-1), m = REALIZED minutes of the scored game, renormalized within team-game. Identical for every source.
  • Identical scale fit. b and per-season HCA fit per walk-forward block on strictly-prior games, IDENTICALLY for every arm (except the labelled internal_b1). Walk-forward: SD.walk_forward_blocks + SD.burn_ord_for (imported); train pool = prior seasons in window + current-season-to-date. (261 folds.)
  • Common scored set. common across arms (a game is scored only if every arm has a rating for it), so per-season RMSEs share a denominator.
  • Missing players. imputed at the source's as-of 10th percentile over the league-active pool (players with minutes in the previous 15 league game dates, read off the games spine). The CANDIDATE pool is identical for every source; the percentile is then taken over each source's own VALUED subset of that pool, so the denominators differ where a source cannot value an active player (see coverage_diagnostics.active_pool_valued_share). What is identical across sources is the scored-player set of every team-game, never a source's percentile denominator. Covered minute share, and its tail, are reported per source per season.
  • As-of rule. latest value at ordinal <= D-1 for all four sources (board: latest day-file ordinal < D, which is the same rule).
  • Not an out-of-sample forecast. Realized minutes are used for ALL arms, matching the production internal convention. These numbers are therefore a MODEL comparison under a shared minutes oracle, not out-of-sample forecasts; absolute RMSE here is optimistic for every arm alike. The pre-game-minutes version of the same question is the published epm_anchor row.
